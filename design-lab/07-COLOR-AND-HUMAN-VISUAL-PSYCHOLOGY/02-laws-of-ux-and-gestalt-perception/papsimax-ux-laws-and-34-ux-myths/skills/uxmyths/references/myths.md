# The 34 UX Myths

Each entry: the popular belief, why it's wrong, and the practical takeaway. Written as an original synthesis of the myths catalogued by Zoltán Gócza and Zoltán Kollin on uxmyths.com, not a copy of their text.

---

### Myth 1, "People read on the web"
**Belief:** Visitors read your copy top to bottom like a book.
**Reality:** People scan for keywords, headings, and scannable chunks; they only read word-for-word once they've found what they were looking for. Eye-tracking studies put average on-page reading well under a quarter of the text.
**Takeaway:** Write for scanning, short paragraphs, meaningful headings, front-loaded key info. Long blocks of promotional or explanatory text get skipped.

### Myth 2, "All pages should be reachable in 3 clicks"
**Belief:** Users abandon a site if a page takes more than 3 clicks/taps to reach.
**Reality:** Click count itself doesn't predict satisfaction or success, several studies found *more* clicks (with clearer intermediate choices) outperformed fewer, confusing ones.
**Takeaway:** Optimize for confident, well-labeled steps toward the goal, not for minimizing a raw click count.

### Myth 3, "People don't scroll"
**Belief:** Everything important must fit above the fold.
**Reality:** Scrolling is now completely natural; large-scale analytics show most attention and engagement happens below the fold, and users scroll when content earns it.
**Takeaway:** Don't cram content to avoid scrolling, but the top of the page still gets the most attention, so it must earn the scroll.

### Myth 4, "Design is about making it look good"
**Belief:** Design = visual polish.
**Reality:** Design is about how something works as much as how it looks, form serves function. Good design is often invisible because it just works.
**Takeaway:** Judge a design by whether it helps users accomplish their goal, not only by its visual appeal.

### Myth 5, "Accessibility is expensive and difficult"
**Belief:** Making a product accessible requires a costly parallel effort.
**Reality:** Designed in from the start, accessibility costs about the same as inaccessible design, separating content from presentation is the core discipline, not an add-on. Retrofitting later is what gets expensive.
**Takeaway:** Bake accessibility into the initial design process; it also tends to improve SEO, performance, and overall usability.

### Myth 6, "Accessible sites are ugly"
**Belief:** Accessibility constraints force a bland, stripped-down look.
**Reality:** Accessibility governs how content is structured and exposed (semantic markup, alternative access paths), not how it's styled. Visual design and accessibility are independent axes.
**Takeaway:** You can, and should, pursue both a strong visual identity and full accessibility at once.

### Myth 7, "Graphics make an element more visible"
**Belief:** Wrapping something important in a flashy, colorful graphic will draw attention to it.
**Reality:** Users have learned to tune out anything that looks like an ad, a phenomenon called banner blindness. Graphic-heavy treatment often makes critical content *less* noticed, not more.
**Takeaway:** Use restrained contrast and text-based emphasis for important content; avoid ad-like visual patterns for anything users actually need to see.

### Myth 8, "Stock photos improve the experience"
**Belief:** Decorative photography makes a page more appealing and trustworthy.
**Reality:** Eye-tracking shows purely decorative images are largely ignored, and generic stock photography can even read as filler or reduce credibility.
**Takeaway:** Only use imagery that carries real information (product, people, data); cut anything purely ornamental.

### Myth 9, "Design has to be original"
**Belief:** Reusing familiar UI patterns is uncreative or lazy.
**Reality:** Established interface conventions work precisely because users already know them, no learning curve required. Novelty for its own sake adds friction.
**Takeaway:** Default to well-tested patterns; only break convention when you're confident the new approach is a genuine improvement, not just different.

### Myth 10, "If the design is good, small details don't matter"
**Belief:** Micro-details (wording, error messages, minor layout choices) are negligible next to the "big" design decisions.
**Reality:** Small changes, a clearer optional-field label, a better-worded error message, a de-emphasized signup step, have repeatedly driven measurable, sometimes very large, gains in conversion and satisfaction.
**Takeaway:** Treat copy, microcopy, and small interaction details as first-class design work, not afterthoughts.

### Myth 11, "You need to redesign periodically"
**Belief:** A periodic full visual overhaul keeps a product competitive.
**Reality:** Users are creatures of habit and often resent sweeping redesigns, even objectively better ones, because they disrupt learned navigation. Sites that stay successful long-term tend to evolve incrementally instead.
**Takeaway:** Prefer continuous, incremental refinement (realigning) over big-bang redesigns; reserve major overhauls for when the underlying model has genuinely stopped working.

### Myth 12, "More choices/features means more satisfaction"
**Belief:** Users are happier with a richer feature set and more options.
**Reality:** Before using a product, people say they want more choice; after using it, usability and simplicity drive actual satisfaction. Too many options create decision paralysis (see Hick's Law).
**Takeaway:** Resist feature creep. Default to fewer, well-chosen options and smart defaults rather than exposing every possible choice.

### Myth 13, "Icons enhance usability"
**Belief:** Icon-only controls are cleaner and just as clear as labeled ones.
**Reality:** Most icons have no universally agreed meaning and are hard to memorize; classic case studies (e.g. toolbar redesigns) show usage jumps dramatically once text labels are added, while re-arranging or re-drawing icons alone does little.
**Takeaway:** Pair icons with text labels except for a small set of near-universal symbols (play/pause, print, close, share); when in doubt, a label alone beats an icon alone.

### Myth 14, "You are like your users"
**Belief:** Because you understand the product deeply, your intuitions about usability transfer to your users.
**Reality:** Building or working on a product makes you atypical by definition, the "false-consensus" bias makes it easy to assume others think and want what you do.
**Takeaway:** Validate assumptions with real user research and testing rather than personal intuition, especially for anything outside your own expertise or context.

### Myth 15, "Users make optimal choices"
**Belief:** Given a page, users evaluate all options and pick the objectively best one.
**Reality:** Users "satisfice", they take the first option that seems good enough, because guessing wrong and going back is cheaper than reading everything carefully first.
**Takeaway:** Make the *correct* option also the most obviously labeled and prominent one; don't rely on users carefully comparing alternatives.

### Myth 16, "Search solves navigation problems"
**Belief:** A good search box compensates for weak information architecture.
**Reality:** Most users click familiar-looking links before they resort to typing a query, recognition beats recall. Search tends to get used mainly for known-item lookups (a specific title, SKU, etc.).
**Takeaway:** Invest in clear navigation and labeling first; treat search as a complement for known-item retrieval, not a substitute for IA.

### Myth 17, "The homepage is your most important page"
**Belief:** The homepage deserves the majority of design attention because it's everyone's entry point.
**Reality:** On most sites the homepage's share of total pageviews has fallen steadily as search, social, and deep-linking send visitors directly to interior pages.
**Takeaway:** Give real design and content attention to the pages people actually land on and use, not just the front door.

### Myth 18, "Flash was evil" *(historical)*
**Belief:** Flash was inherently unusable and bad for the web.
**Reality:** Mature Flash implementations could be accessible, SEO-friendly, and support standard browser navigation; its real death sentence was mobile devices simply not supporting it.
**Takeaway:** The general lesson survives the specific technology: judge a tool by how well an implementation follows usability fundamentals, not by blanket reputation, and today, that also means designing for the platforms your users are actually on.

### Myth 19, "You don't need content to design a website"
**Belief:** Layout can be designed first with placeholder ("lorem ipsum") text, content added later.
**Reality:** Designs built around filler text make unrealistic assumptions about length, tone, and structure, and often break or feel hollow once real content goes in. Users come for content, not chrome.
**Takeaway:** Design with real (or realistic draft) content from the start; treat copywriting as part of interface design, not a step that happens after.

### Myth 20, "If it works for Amazon, it will work for you"
**Belief:** Copying a proven pattern from a market leader guarantees the same result.
**Reality:** A pattern's success depends on context, traffic volume, brand trust, audience size, and business model, that rarely transfers. The same review feature that thrives on Amazon can flop on a smaller, less-trusted site.
**Takeaway:** Study *why* a pattern worked for someone else before adopting it, and validate it against your own users and context rather than assuming it will transfer.

### Myth 21, "People can tell you what they want"
**Belief:** Asking users what they'd like changed is a reliable way to guide design decisions.
**Reality:** People are confident but frequently wrong about their own future behavior, famous product research failures (e.g. New Coke, early negative reactions to products that later became icons) show stated preference diverging sharply from actual behavior.
**Takeaway:** Treat what users *say* as one input; weight what they actually *do* (usage data, behavioral testing) much more heavily when the two conflict.

### Myth 22, "Usability testing is expensive"
**Belief:** Proper usability testing needs a dedicated lab, many participants, and weeks of time.
**Reality:** Small, informal tests, even paper prototypes with five or so participants, reliably surface the majority of major usability problems at a fraction of the cost.
**Takeaway:** Run cheap, frequent, small-sample tests throughout a project rather than skipping testing because a "proper" study feels out of budget.

### Myth 23, "Choices should be limited to 7±2"
**Belief:** Menus and lists should never exceed about seven items, per Miller's "magic number."
**Reality:** Miller's original research was about short-term memory recall, not about visually scannable information already displayed on screen; broad, shallow navigation with many visible options often outperforms deep, narrow menus.
**Takeaway:** Don't artificially cap on-screen choices at seven; instead group and label them well, and trim options based on actual necessity, not a memorized rule of thumb.

### Myth 24, "People use your product exactly as you intended"
**Belief:** Once shipped, a feature will be used the way its designers imagined.
**Reality:** Users routinely repurpose products in unplanned ways, typing full URLs into a search box, using SMS built for network alerts to text each other, or using a media-focused product mainly for something else entirely.
**Takeaway:** Watch actual usage patterns after launch; unexpected use is often a signal of real, unmet needs worth designing for rather than "fixing."

### Myth 25, "Aesthetics don't matter if usability is good"
**Belief:** A functionally usable interface doesn't need to look good.
**Reality:** Visual appeal shapes perceived credibility and even perceived usability, attractive interfaces are trusted more and *feel* easier to use, independent of objective performance.
**Takeaway:** Invest in visual design alongside usability; a credible first impression is often what earns the user's patience to discover the underlying functionality at all.

### Myth 26, "Usability testing equals focus groups"
**Belief:** Gathering a group to discuss their opinions is the same research method as a usability test.
**Reality:** Focus groups capture stated opinions and attitudes (what people *say*); usability tests observe actual task performance (what people *do*), different goals, different methods, typically different project phases.
**Takeaway:** Use focus groups early to explore attitudes and audience; use one-on-one usability testing to validate whether an interface actually works.

### Myth 27, "UX design is about usability"
**Belief:** A usable interface is the full scope of user experience design.
**Reality:** Usability (can people accomplish the task?) is table stakes; UX also encompasses delight, emotional response, meaning, and engagement, qualities usability metrics alone don't capture.
**Takeaway:** Design for feeling, not just for task completion, pleasant surprise, personality, and flow contribute to experience quality beyond raw efficiency.

### Myth 28, "White space is wasted space"
**Belief:** Empty space is unused real estate that should be filled with more content or controls.
**Reality:** White space actively improves readability, comprehension, and visual hierarchy, and contributes to perceived quality and brand positioning.
**Takeaway:** Treat white space as a functional design element, not a gap to be filled, cutting it to cram in more content usually hurts comprehension.

### Myth 29, "People are rational"
**Belief:** Users weigh costs and benefits logically before deciding.
**Reality:** Decades of behavioral-economics research (anchoring, framing, loss aversion, decoy effects, the pull of "free") show decisions are driven far more by predictable emotional and cognitive shortcuts than by careful calculation.
**Takeaway:** Design decision points (pricing, defaults, framing of options) with these predictable biases in mind rather than assuming purely logical evaluation.

### Myth 30, "Experts don't need to test their designs"
**Belief:** An experienced designer's review can substitute for testing with real users.
**Reality:** Expert review and usability testing surface different classes of problems; even seasoned experts are frequently surprised by how real users actually behave.
**Takeaway:** Use expert review to catch known usability violations early, but still validate with real user testing before and after, the two methods complement rather than replace each other.

### Myth 31, "UX design is one step in the project"
**Belief:** UX design is a discrete phase, typically the wireframing step, sandwiched between requirements and development.
**Reality:** User-centered design properly spans strategy, research, interface design, and post-launch iteration; treating it as a single deliverable-producing phase misses most of its value.
**Takeaway:** Involve UX thinking from strategy/discovery through post-launch iteration, not just during the interface-design step.

### Myth 32, "Success happens overnight"
**Belief:** Breakout products succeed suddenly, as if from nowhere.
**Reality:** Products widely seen as "instant" hits, iPod, Gmail, Twitter, Amazon, typically took years of iteration, false starts, and internal skepticism before traction appeared.
**Takeaway:** Set expectations (yours and stakeholders') for sustained iteration; don't judge a young product's design as failed just because it hasn't broken out yet.

### Myth 33, "Mobile users are distracted"
**Belief:** Mobile usage happens on the go, in short, scattered, low-attention bursts.
**Reality:** Most mobile usage actually happens at home or work, in the same "distracted" environment as desktop and TV use, distraction is a general condition, not a mobile-specific one.
**Takeaway:** Don't design mobile experiences as if users are always rushed or inattentive; design for real context, which is often just as settled as desktop use.

### Myth 34, "Simple means minimal"
**Belief:** Reducing visible elements (fewer buttons, hidden menus, sparse UI) makes a product simple to use.
**Reality:** Simplicity is about reducing the user's cognitive and interaction effort; a visually minimal interface can hide complexity and increase friction (e.g. ambiguous icon-only controls, hamburger menus with poor discoverability).
**Takeaway:** Optimize for actual ease of use and clarity, not for visual sparseness, sometimes a visible label, button, or option makes an interface *more* simple to use, not less.
