# Ethics checklist for behaviour-shaping design

From chapter 11 of Yablonski's *Laws of UX* (first edition, pp. 107 to 119). The seven mechanisms and the three practices are the book's. The test questions and the gamification notes are additions, and are marked as such.

## The premise

Knowing how people perceive and decide makes it possible to help them, and equally possible to work against them. The chapter's position:

- Using psychology in design is not wrong in itself.
- Harm does not need intent. Features built in good faith have produced compulsive use and worse, and the builder still answers for it.
- The business goal (time on site, daily active users) is seldom the same as the person's goal (get something done). The designer stands between the two and may be the only one arguing for the person.

## The one question

**Addition.** Before any mechanism below is used, ask:

> Does this help the person do what they came to do, or does it serve a number of ours at their expense?

If the honest answer is the second, do not ship it. If the answer is "both", write down what the person gets, and check it against the tests below.

## Seven mechanisms

For each: what it is, the book's example, and a test.

### 1. Intermittent variable rewards (p. 110)

- **What.** Reward that arrives unpredictably produces the most persistent behaviour, more than reward every time. This is the slot machine.
- **Book example.** Pull-to-refresh on a feed.
- **Test (addition).** Is the unpredictability the point of the feature, or a side effect of real content arriving? Would the feature still be worth using if the reward were predictable?

### 2. Infinite loops (p. 111)

- **What.** Autoplay and endless scroll remove the moment where a person would decide to continue.
- **Book example.** The next video playing by itself.
- **Test (addition).** Is there a natural stopping point, and does the product respect it? Can the person see how much is left?

### 3. Social affirmation (p. 111)

- **What.** Likes and positive comments satisfy the need for approval and belonging, briefly, and bring people back to check.
- **Book example.** The like button.
- **Test (addition).** Does the feature compare people, or put a number on a person's worth? Can someone take part without being ranked?

### 4. Defaults (p. 112)

- **What.** Most people never change a default, so the default decides for them. One study found default privacy settings matched what users expected 37% of the time.
- **Test (addition).** Is every default the one the person would pick if asked? A default that favours the product over the person is a dark pattern with good manners.

### 5. Lack of friction (p. 113)

- **What.** The easier an action, the more it happens and the sooner it becomes habit. Removing friction is how every other law in the book helps people. It is also how to get people to do things they did not mean to.
- **Book example.** One-press ordering buttons.
- **Test (addition).** Is the friction being removed in the person's way, or was it protecting them? Purchases, deletions, sharing and anything public deserve a moment of friction.

### 6. Reciprocity (p. 114)

- **What.** People feel obliged to return a gesture.
- **Book example.** Being told someone endorsed you, which prompts an endorsement back.
- **Test (addition).** Is the gesture real and from a person, or generated by the product to create an obligation?

### 7. Dark patterns (p. 114)

- **What.** Designs that get people to do what they did not intend: buy more, share more, accept marketing. A 2019 crawl of about 11,000 shopping sites found 1,818 instances.
- **Book example.** A low-stock message that appears whatever the stock.
- **Test (addition).** Is every claim on the screen true? Is declining as easy and as neutral as accepting?

## Three practices (pp. 118 to 119)

1. **Think beyond the happy path.** Design the non-ideal scenarios first: the person who is behind, who failed, who is alone in their cohort, who returns after a gap. Products that only fit the ideal case leave everyone else exposed.
2. **Diversify teams and thinking.** A team that shares one background misses the same things. Do not limit personas to the core segment.
3. **Look beyond data.** Numbers show what people do. They do not show why, or what the product is doing to their lives. Talk to people.

## Notes for gamified and engagement features

**Addition.** These mechanisms overlap with the Octalysis core drives. The overlap is where to look hardest.

| Mechanism | Closest Octalysis drive | Honest form | Line not to cross |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variable rewards | 7. Unpredictability and Curiosity | New content worth finding | Randomness added to pull people back |
| Infinite loops | 7, and 2 without an end | A clear next step | No stopping point, hidden length |
| Social affirmation | 5. Social Influence and Relatedness | Shared goals, recognition of real work | Ranking people, public shortfall |
| Defaults | 4. Ownership and Possession | Defaults the person would choose | Opt-outs buried, sharing on by default |
| Lack of friction | 2. Development and Accomplishment | Fewer steps to the person's goal | Fewer steps to our goal |
| Reciprocity | 5 | Real gestures between people | Manufactured obligations |
| Scarcity (dark pattern) | 6. Scarcity and Impatience | A real deadline or a real limit | A false one |
| Loss framing | 8. Loss and Avoidance | Showing what real progress would be lost | Punishing a missed day, guilt copy |

Streaks deserve their own mention. A streak uses progress (drive 2) on the way up and loss (drive 8) on the way down. The moment it breaks is a strong negative peak under the Peak-End Rule, and it is where people quit. Decide what a person sees at that moment before building the streak.

## Use in a review

**Addition.**

- Any feature meant to bring people back, keep them longer or raise completion gets this checklist.
- A feature that fails a test is reported as a finding whatever the rubric says. Ethics lines are non-negotiables, not trade-offs.
