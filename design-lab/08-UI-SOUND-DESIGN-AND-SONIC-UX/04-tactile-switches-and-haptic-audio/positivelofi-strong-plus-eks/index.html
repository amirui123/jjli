An Accident That Sounded Like a String
Around 1978-79, Alex Strong — a CS student at Stanford who also played viola — was experimenting with wavetable synthesis on an 8-bit microprocessor. He tried filtering the wavetable on each reading pass: average each sample with its neighbor. A two-point average. No multiplications. The result sounded like a plucked string. Not a simulation — something alive, with natural decay, harmonics fading in the right order. "Almost no matter what initial table was used," the paper would say. Even random noise sounded like a string.

This was the accident. Strong brought it to Karplus, who did the math. They built the Digitar VLSI chip (reportedly 16 voices, Intel 8080A/TI TMS9900) and published in 1983. Neither had set out to synthesize a string.
The Composer Who Made It Music
David A. Jaffe was a composition PhD at CCRMA who played in the same string quartet as Strong. He brought the algorithm to Julius O. Smith III, who recognized something extraordinary: the KS transfer function coincided exactly with that of an ideal vibrating string. Karplus and Strong had suspected this — Smith demonstrated that the KS transfer function corresponded to an idealized vibrating string model. Together Jaffe and Smith developed the Extended KS (EKS) algorithm — six filters: H_eta (tuning), H_beta (pick position), H_L (dynamic level), H_p (pick direction), H_s (stiffness), H_d (loop gain). Published 1983, same issue of Computer Music Journal.
Silicon Valley Breakdown — Imaginary Instruments
Jaffe's Silicon Valley Breakdown (1982, premiered Venice Biennale 1983) — an orchestra of imaginary plucked string instruments, from a "piccolo mandolin" to an immense bass "plucked Golden Gate Bridge." Four ensembles in quadraphonic space. Hailed by Le Monde. Performed in 28+ countries. Jaffe described it as blurring "the dividing line between string resonance and reverberation, between instrument and space" — the seed of commuted synthesis. → Piccolo Mandolin → Plucked Gate → Quadraspace → Venice Biennale
The NeXT Machine — Two Revolutions, One Black Cube
Steve Jobs recruited Jaffe and Smith (1986-91) to build the NeXT Music Kit — first demonstrated October 12, 1988 at Davies Symphony Hall with a live duet between the Cube and an SF Symphony violinist. Independently, Tim Berners-Lee built WorldWideWeb.app on a NeXT Cube at CERN in September 1990 — in Objective-C on NeXTSTEP, the same language and OS as the Music Kit. Two revolutions, one machine. Both open-sourced within 12 months: Music Kit → CCRMA 1992, WWW → public domain April 30, 1993. → WWW·89 → Jobs Demo '88 → Public Domain '93
Commuted Synthesis — The Room IS the String
Smith's insight (1992): since both string and body are linear time-invariant systems, their order can be commuted. The body IR is folded into the excitation — the string plucks a pre-convolved body. In Strong+, the BODY section applies an impulse response before the feedback loop. The reverb is not an effect: it is the instrument body. ROOM, SPRING, CAVE, PLATE, NANO, CATHEDRAL, TITAN, VOID, WOOD, METAL — ten different physical structures. → Commuted Body
Vibe Coding — A New Kind of Making
Strong+ was built through dialogue between Yuwa (musician, composer, creator of Positive Lofi YouTube channel, passionate amateur of synthesis) and Claude (Anthropic). Yuwa is not a programmer — he is someone who loves sound deeply and wanted to understand what happens inside a physical string model when you pluck it. The same question Alex Strong was asking in 1978.

The NeXT parallel: Jaffe/Smith built the Music Kit on NeXT hardware. Berners-Lee built the web browser on the same machine, same language (Objective-C), same years. Both released freely. Strong+ inherits this lineage: algorithm public domain, Music Kit open-sourced, web made distribution free, vibe coding makes building accessible. The same compression, applied to creation itself.

Authorship: Yuwa brought curiosity, musical ear, direction, corrections. Claude brought implementation, research, code. Knowledge distributed across the conversation — exactly as in 1983, where Strong discovered, Karplus analyzed, Jaffe musicalized, and Smith proved. The original patents have expired and the algorithm is freely implementable. The history is documented. The sounds are yours.
Step 1 — The Simplest Possible Loop
A circular buffer of N samples filled with white noise. On each cycle, replace each sample with the average of itself and its neighbor. That is the entire Karplus-Strong algorithm.
y[n] = 0.5 * (y[n-N] + y[n-N-1])
The averaging filter attenuates high frequencies progressively — the string loses upper harmonics first, exactly as a real string does. Pitch = sampleRate / N. No multiplications — designed for 8-bit CPUs.
Step 2 — EKS Controls
Loop Gain (H_d) — energy retained per cycle. 0.999 = long decay, 0.982 = short. This is your sustain control.

Material (one-zero weight) — 0.5 = original KS, bright. Towards 1 = dark (nylon/gut). Towards 0 = bright (steel).

Dynamic Level (H_L) — lowpass bandwidth on excitation. Loud=bright. Soft=dark. Velocity-sensitive.

Pick Position (H_beta) — comb filter. Near bridge = nasal. Over soundhole = warm. Cancels harmonics at pick node.

Pick Direction (H_p) — one-pole LP on excitation. Shapes attack character.
Step 3 — The Tuning Problem (H_eta)
N must be an integer, but the true period is fractional. At 44100 Hz, A=440 gives N=100.227. A first-order allpass introduces a sub-sample delay that compensates the pitch error.
H_eta(z) = -(eta - z^-1) / (1 - eta * z^-1)
Step 4 — Commuted Synthesis
String and body are both linear time-invariant systems — their order can be swapped. Fold the body IR into the excitation. The string plucks a pre-convolved body. No separate reverb step. In Strong+: the BODY section IS the instrument body, not an effect insert.
Step 5 — Sympathetic Strings
A second KS delay line tuned at an interval (unison+detune, fifth, octave) coupled to the first via shared energy. A sitar has sympathetic drone strings. A piano has undamped strings that resonate. In Strong+: SYMPATHETIC section — tune offset, decay, and mix of the secondary string.
Bonus — The Drum Algorithm
Karplus & Strong's original paper also described a drum variant: with probability (1-b), flip the sign of the averaged sample. At b=0.5: pure noise/drum. At b=1: pure string. In between: indefinite-pitch percussion. The boundary between string and drum is a single probability value.
Alex Strong
INVENTOR · VIOLIST · BLUEGRASS MUSICIAN
CS student at Stanford and practicing violist. Invented the algorithm c.1978-79 while experimenting with wavetable modification on an 8-bit microprocessor. The two-point average was a surprise — the string sound was not the goal. Co-designed the Digitar VLSI chip (16 voices, Intel 8080A). Later published a superior method as a patent (never commercialized).
Kevin Karplus
ANALYST · COMPUTER SCIENTIST · STANFORD
Performed the first mathematical analysis using Z-transforms. Co-designed the Digitar chip. Their 1983 paper in Computer Music Journal (MIT Press, vol.7 no.2) established the algorithm formally. Later moved to bioinformatics at UC Santa Cruz.
David A. Jaffe
COMPOSER · CCRMA STANFORD · b. 1955
Composition PhD at CCRMA, played in the same string quartet as Strong. Gave KS its first musical voice in May All Your Children Be Acrobats (1981). Created Silicon Valley Breakdown (1982) — the landmark work. With Smith developed EKS. Built the NeXT Music Kit for Steve Jobs (1986-91). Over 90 compositions.
Julius O. Smith III
THEORIST · DSP ENGINEER · PROFESSOR EMERITUS CCRMA
Proved that KS = transfer function of an ideal vibrating string. Derived KS as a special case of digital waveguide synthesis. 1983 PhD thesis applied theory to violin synthesis. Formalized commuted synthesis (1992). Co-invented the audio fingerprinting algorithm behind Shazam. CCRMA textbooks are the definitive reference.

The Original Algorithm (Karplus & Strong 1983)
Initialize N samples with white noise. On each cycle, average each sample with its neighbor and write back.
y[n] = 0.5 * (y[n-N] + y[n-N-1])
H(z) = 0.5 + 0.5*z-1 — one-zero lowpass. Attenuates highs progressively. N = sampleRate / pitch. No multiplications — 8-bit compatible.→ King Strong→ Transfer Function
Extended KS Filters (Jaffe & Smith 1983)
H_p(z) = (1-p)/(1-p*z-1) pick direction LP H_beta(z) = 1 - z^(-floor(beta*N+0.5)) pick position comb H_L(z) = (1-R_L)/(1-R_L*z-1) dynamic level LP H_eta(z) = -(eta-z-1)/(1-eta*z-1) fractional delay tuning H_s(z) = allpass chain stiffness / dispersion H_d(z) = g * one-zero filter loop gain + decay
Commuted Synthesis (Smith 1992)
String and body are both linear time-invariant systems — their order can be commuted. Fold the body IR into the excitation. Eliminates hundreds of resonator filter sections. In Strong+: the BODY section IS the instrument body — applied before the feedback loop, not after.→ Commuted Body→ Wave Equation
Digital Waveguide Synthesis
Smith showed KS is a degenerate case of a bidirectional digital waveguide — two delay lines propagating traveling waves in opposite directions. The full theory models strings, tubes, membranes, vocal tracts. Yamaha licensed waveguide patents from Stanford 1989 (Sondius). All original patents expired ~2010 — the algorithm is freely implementable.
